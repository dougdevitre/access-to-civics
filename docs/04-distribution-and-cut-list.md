# 04 — Distribution and the cut list

The remaining risk is not design quality. It's **distribution and scope**.

## The wedge: Constitution Day, September 17

Section 111 of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2005 (Pub. L. 108-447) requires each
educational institution receiving federal funds to hold an educational program on the U.S.
Constitution on September 17 each year. The Department of Education implemented it by notice in
May 2005 (70 FR 29727). A January 2025 federal directive reiterated the requirement and
instructed agencies to monitor and verify compliance. "Educational institution" is read broadly —
preschools through higher ed, public and any federally funded private.

Every district in the country has to do *something* on one specific day, most scramble for
material, and there is an established scramble-relief channel: constitutiondayhub.org positions
itself as a one-stop shop of free, nonpartisan, age-appropriate Constitution lessons, and USED
maintains its own resource page.

**Be honest about the fit.** The mandate is the *U.S.* Constitution, so a pure state-constitution
game does not discharge it. Ship a 45-minute Constitution Day module that runs the federal
convention **and** ends with the bridge — *"your state wrote one too, and it's four times longer;
here's why."* Better lesson than either alone, satisfies the mandate, and gives us a hard annual
date for launches, press, and partner outreach.

**Action:** get listed on the hub and the USED resource page before September 17.

## Signature metric: changed their mind

Every civics product measures recall. Recall is the least interesting thing civics teaches and
the easiest to fake.

Instrument **vote-switching**: log when a kid votes one way, hears the opposing constituency's
argument, and switches. That yields:

- a defensible outcome claim ("students changed position after argument in N% of contested clauses")
- a genuine research contribution nobody else has
- a metric nearly impossible to game by teaching to the test

It is also the honest goal of the product: civic humility, not civic trivia.

Secondary measurable claims for a pre/post instrument:
1. Can locate a specific right in their own state's constitution.
2. Can articulate the strongest argument for a rule they voted against.

Without an instrument, districts file this under "fun activity" and it never gets budget.

## Funding path

State bar foundations / IOLTA, state supreme court civics offices, Secretaries of State, and NEH
are the realistic sources. A state bar sponsoring "the Missouri edition" is a cleaner fit than a
district purchase order and it arrives with the LRE distribution channel attached.

## Cut list

**Keep for v1**
- Missouri + one contrast state
- The Draft → Mirror loop
- Asymmetric goal cards
- Ratification vote
- The Constitution Day module
- Offline PWA
- Printed charter

**Cut or defer**
- Tribal constitutions (do it right later, or not at all)
- Local charters
- Lineage/diff viewer
- Act II "live under your own rule"
- Open dataset release
- MCP server
- 48 more states

**Cut permanently unless something changes**
- **The three-decade multi-outcome simulation.** It's the most expensive thing in the plan, it's
  where neutrality risk concentrates, and the negotiation + ratification mechanic delivers most of
  the learning without it. Test whether the game still works with consequences reduced to a single
  citizen letter and no scoreboard at all. If it does — and it probably does — that version ships
  this year.

## Two things that will quietly kill it

1. **A dominant strategy.** If one charter reliably wins, we've built propaganda with a
   scoreboard. Test it: 10,000 simulated playthroughs, check win distribution across archetypes.
2. **No measurable outcome.** See the signature metric above.
